# Design Ethics

Use for consequential choices, data or permissions, prices and subscriptions, exit routes, or claims establishing trust. A routine control with settled consequences does not need a separate ethics workflow.

## Identify affected choices and risks

Identify what the person wants to accomplish, what the product asks them to accept, who benefits, and who bears relevant cost or risk: money, data, attention, work, effects on others, or access to services.

A recorded click does not prove understanding or preference. User agency means being able to understand choices, decline, reconsider, and act within legitimate product and role limits. It does not require unlimited options.

Name conflicts between business objectives and user interests. Recommend an approach preserving legitimate objectives without concealing consequences. Sensitive contexts, including children's services, may require relevant expertise; do not invent universal age thresholds or transfer adult patterns unchanged.

Aim for trust justified by actual capabilities. Credible appearance or increased expressed trust alone does not establish reliability.

## Identify deceptive patterns

Identify the affected choice, mechanism, and observed or plausible consequence before applying a pattern label. A screenshot does not establish intent. Distinguish demonstrated mismatches, risks needing behavioral evidence, and preferences.

| Mechanism | Design response | Boundary |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Material cost, commitment, or data use is missing or delayed | Put it in the decision context | Optional supporting detail can remain layered |
| Apparent control meaning differs from its effect | Align label, default, appearance, and outcome | Familiar appearance does not prove familiar behavior |
| Declining, changing, or leaving has unexplained burden | Remove irrelevant steps and provide a usable route | Genuine identity checks or different consequences may justify different steps |
| Unrelated purchase, marketing, or permission is bundled | Separate independent choices | Explain necessary dependencies |
| Pressure uses false scarcity, resetting deadlines, or unsupported social proof | Remove invented circumstances | Accurate availability limits and recommendations can remain |
| Repetition, guilt, or misleading emphasis undermines refusal | Respect the decision and allow later reconsideration | A materially changed need can justify a new request |

Fix the behavior that misleads or obstructs users. A policy link or warning cannot fix an unusable way to decline.

A [CHI 2026 systematic review](https://dl.acm.org/doi/full/10.1145/3772318.3790383) found context-dependent behavioral effects across tested deceptive designs and limits to tested disclosure interventions. Its privacy/commerce emphasis and short-term studies do not establish a universal ranking or make every prominent button deceptive.

## Explain choices before action

Before users act, explain what changes, who is affected, when it happens, relevant costs, required disclosures, and whether it can be reversed. Put important charges or access changes beside the choice; detailed terms can provide more information.

Alternatives must be perceivable and operable through relevant access modes. Primary and secondary actions can differ visually; identical button sizes or colors are not a universal ethical test. Check whether people can actually find and understand the alternative.

Separate required from optional choices. Explain genuine dependencies and preserve unaffected functionality where feasible. Do not promise an alternative the product lacks or use vague security claims to justify unrelated collection.

Choose defaults with attention to consequence, reversibility, established preferences, and applicable requirements. Make consequential defaults understandable and changeable. Inaction is not proof of informed preference, and preselection does not itself establish valid consent.

Explain recommendations when the basis matters: “For teams with shared approvals” is more useful than an unsupported “Best.” Paid placement should not masquerade as independent advice. Trivial choices do not need mandatory explanations.

## Explain permissions and data use

Distinguish platform permission, product preference, and legal basis for processing. Technical access does not itself establish legal consent; a product toggle cannot override platform denial.

Connect requests to the task, practical scope, purpose, and material sharing or retention consequences. Request necessary access without unrelated collection. For optional access, permit refusal; explain affected functionality and genuine alternatives without repeated prompts.

Time requests so their purpose is understandable. A preceding click or extra dialog does not prove comprehension.

Provide a usable way to inspect and change relevant preferences. Distinguish future collection, existing data deletion, marketing preferences, and account access. Confirm actual revocation timing, propagation, and erasure behavior with Engineering before promising them.

## Explain pricing and cancellation

Before commitment, communicate relevant price, billing period, total commitment, renewal, trial conversion, inclusions, optional extras, and restrictions. Distinguish monthly comparison prices from annual amounts actually billed.

If a total depends on missing information, explain the dependency and update the amount before commitment. Never invent fees, tax, stock, deadlines, refunds, or policies.

Make exit discoverable where people manage the relationship. Distinguish stopping renewal, ending access now, requesting a refund, deleting an account, and erasing data. Show actual effective dates, remaining access, charges, and recovery limits.

An optional retention offer can coexist with an obvious cancellation route. Repeated offers, unrelated mandatory surveys, resets, or broken support dependencies can obstruct exit. Explain necessary authentication or human handling and consider a less burdensome protection. Equal click counts alone do not establish fairness.

Confirmation must describe the real result: request received, renewal stopped, access ended, and refund issued are different outcomes. See [cancel, undo, and compensation](interaction-design.md#distinguish-cancel-undo-and-compensation).

## Use friction proportionately

Identify the harm before adding confirmation, delay, reauthentication, or constraints. Consider impact, scope, reversibility, accidental activation, affected people, accessibility, and urgent use.

Compare clearer scope, previews, safer defaults, and reliable recovery. Shared-workspace deletion may justify reviewing affected resources; reversible preferences usually need less interruption. Typed phrases and timers are not universal safeguards.

Apply safeguards according to risk. Do not add obstacles only to options the provider dislikes. More steps or a Confirm click do not prove understanding or excuse unclear design.

## Support trust with evidence

Use qualifications, reviews, guarantees, certification, comparison claims, and trust marks only with an established basis, scope, and permission. Do not invent customers, testimonials, security assurances, compliance, or live activity. Distinguish drafting placeholders from publishable claims.

Where useful, let people inspect the basis: issuer, applicable scope, current status, and actual guarantee. A lock icon, badge, or professional layout does not prove security or lawful processing.

Keep messages and outcomes consistent. Declined options should not silently return; “private” must match behavior; deletion notices must not exceed confirmed erasure. Check the underlying behavior; changing copy alone cannot fix these mismatches.

## Check applicable requirements

When compliance determines the deliverable, establish jurisdiction, users, product category, activity, and current rule status. Check official requirements and relevant interpretation or identify the need for qualified review.

Distinguish law, proposals, nonbinding guidance, and organizational policy. Consent, cancellation, platform, and children's-service requirements differ by scope; do not turn a jurisdiction-specific example into a universal UI rule or rely on a superseded requirement.

General design recommendations are not compliance findings. Verify current official text when a concrete legal obligation matters instead of treating this reference as a legal manual.

## Evaluate user choices

Trace material accepting, declining, changing, and exit paths. Can the person predict consequences, understand the trade-off, locate a legitimate alternative, and obtain the promised outcome?

Inspecting the supplied design can reveal a cost omitted from the decision context or a missing route. Actual billing, preference persistence, permission enforcement, cancellation, and erasure require behavior or implementation evidence.

When participant evaluation is justified, use neutral tasks and check what people expect to happen, pay, share, or retain. Relevant outcomes include unwanted commitments, successful refusal, correction, complaints, and unequal access burden. Conversion and satisfaction alone do not establish autonomy or comprehension.

For refinement, repair the harmful mechanism while preserving legitimate persuasion and identity. For review, distinguish observed defects from unresolved risks and preferences.
