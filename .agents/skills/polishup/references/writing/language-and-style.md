# Language & Style

Use for word choice, sentence clarity, terminology, grammar, punctuation, and local editing. Reuse the established purpose and audience. For larger structural changes, use [Content Structure](content-structure.md); for character and attitude, use [Voice & Tone](voice-and-tone.md).

## Follow language and project conventions

Identify the language variety, register (language appropriate to the setting), formality, project style, and exact terms to preserve. Reuse the project dictionary or style sheet. Otherwise follow consistent existing usage; ask when conflicting conventions affect the result.

Distinguish the reason for an edit:

| Basis | Action |
| --- | --- |
| Error under the intended language convention | Correct it; preserve protected quotations or deliberate character speech |
| Ambiguity that changes meaning or action | Resolve it from the source or ask; do not guess |
| Comprehension problem | Change the construction that causes difficulty |
| House-style preference | Apply the project's choice without declaring other valid forms incorrect |

Preserve intentional dialect, fragments, and literary constructions when they serve the brief. A checker flag or unfamiliar expression does not establish an error.

## Use precise words and consistent terms

Name the actual action, object, degree, or relationship. Prefer common, simple words over rare or formal alternatives when equally precise. Do not choose elaborate wording to sound professional: use "use" instead of "utilize" when no distinction is needed. Retain specialist terminology when readers need it to work, search, or distinguish concepts; explain it where needed. Avoid creating an acronym for a term used only once.

Do not assemble abstract nouns into a label when ordinary wording identifies the same thing. If a new term is useful, define it without presenting it as established terminology. Replace awkward synonyms with the correct domain term; keep exact identifiers and meaningful distinctions.

Replace vague evaluation with supported detail. If the source provides no detail, identify the gap rather than invent a benefit. Check idiom and domain meaning as well as dictionary equivalence. Preserve the scope and commitment: changing "may" to "will" or "some" to "all" changes the claim.

Use one term for one concept. Retain different terms for different concepts; "authentication" and "authorization" are not stylistic alternatives. Keep product names, UI labels, commands, identifiers, placeholders, and defined terms exact when their identity matters. Explain an unfamiliar label rather than silently renaming it. Verify uncertain terminology against an authoritative source.

## Clarify sentence structure

Make the main action and its qualifications easy to identify. Check nested clauses, separated word pairs, stacked modifiers, and strings of nouns with unclear relationships. Move conditions beside the affected claim, explain missing relationships, or split the sentence where the meaning allows.

Use verbs when a noun phrase unnecessarily hides an action: "The team assessed the proposal" is more direct than "The team conducted an assessment of the proposal." Keep a defined noun when the document needs that exact term for an activity or deliverable. Do not invent an actor or object to complete a rewrite.

Choose active or passive voice by responsibility, focus, and what is known. Active voice often identifies responsibility clearly. Passive voice can suit an unknown actor or keep the result in focus. Preserve responsibility when it matters; apply the target language's conventions rather than English word order universally.

## Resolve ambiguity

Inspect pronouns, qualifying phrases, negation, words such as "all" or "only", dates, and conditions. If two plausible readings change the outcome, use the source to resolve them or ask a focused question.

"After Lina sent Maya the draft, she revised it" leaves the actor unclear. Name the actor only if the source identifies her. Likewise, "Only managers can approve refunds" limits who can approve; "Managers can approve only refunds" limits what they can approve.

Keep an obvious pronoun when repeating the noun would add nothing. Preserve intentional ambiguity in requested creative work; resolve accidental ambiguity in instructions and factual claims.

## Remove unnecessary wording

Remove wording that adds no needed meaning, context, courtesy, or emphasis. Add missing conditions, relationships, definitions, or explanations. Short sentences can still be difficult; longer explanations can be necessary.

| Signal | Repair |
| --- | --- |
| Generic opening or announcement of intent | Start with the actual information unless orientation is needed |
| Same point restated without development | Keep one clear statement; retain useful detail or a distinct implication |
| Corporate abstraction or unexplained jargon | Name the supported actor, action, object, or consequence |
| Hyperbole or unverified superiority, speed, or impact | State the supported capability or evidence; do not invent specificity |
| Abstract significance or benefit added to a factual sentence | Verify what the extra claim adds and what supports it; explain, qualify, or remove it |
| Repeated rhetorical questions, stock contrasts, or three-part lists | State the relationship directly; retain a device that contributes meaning |
| Stacked hedges | State what is uncertain and why; preserve necessary qualifications |
| Repeated headings, bold, emoji, or punctuation | Keep formatting that supports navigation, meaning, or an explicit style requirement |

If the only supplied fact is CSV export, replace "Our revolutionary export experience transforms productivity" or "Export records to CSV, ensuring better decisions" with "Export records to CSV." Both versions add an unsupported benefit. Keep an ending phrase when it states a supported purpose, condition, or consequence; an English word ending in "-ing" is not itself a defect.

These signals identify possible communication problems, not AI authorship. For unsupported content, use [Content & Evidence](content-and-evidence.md); for duplicated sections, use [Content Structure](content-structure.md). Do not create a universal banned-word list.

Do not optimize for an AI-detector score. Do not add mistakes, random word substitutions, forced slang, or invented experience to appear human. Judge the result by accuracy, usefulness, context, and the requested voice; preserve deliberate language choices that serve the brief.

## Check grammar and punctuation

Check agreement, tense, aspect, spelling, punctuation, capitalization, and number/date presentation in the intended language and variety. Preserve distinctions that affect meaning. Reuse project conventions for valid alternatives such as spelling variants or serial commas. Record recurring unresolved choices for a substantial document only when needed.

In practical or business copy, prefer digits for quantities, measurements, prices, percentages, and comparisons unless project conventions require otherwise. For example, prefer "7 tools" to "seven tools" in a feature list. Follow the relevant conventions for narrative writing, sentence beginnings, legal wording, and protected strings. Change presentation without changing the value or precision.

Use a current language authority when uncertain. For example, [EYD V](https://ejaan.kemendikdasmen.go.id/eyd/) distinguishes the separate preposition in "di kantor" from the attached prefix in "dijual"; do not apply that distinction to every occurrence of "di".

Apply the [Writing rules](rules.md). Choose other punctuation by the relationship it expresses. Avoid repeated dramatic pauses, stacked asides, and chains of colons or semicolons used for effect. Restructure overloaded sentences without introducing comma splices or altering protected strings. Do not replace every removed em dash with the same punctuation pattern. Flag or ask about a suspected quotation error rather than silently changing attributed words.

## Check flow and readability

Read adjacent sentences for continuity, emphasis, and unintended repetition. Vary construction when it improves the passage; do not randomize sentence lengths or replace precise terms to create variation. Keep useful parallelism, deliberate cadence, and clear long sentences. Reading aloud can reveal awkward phrasing but does not prove comprehension.

Use readability scores and checker suggestions to locate possible problems. Check which language, readers, and features they measure, including how they handle names and technical terms. A length-based score cannot establish accuracy, clear reasoning, or understanding. Do not apply English grade targets to other languages or delete qualifications to improve a score.

Check suggested changes against the original. Can readers identify the same actors, actions, scope, conditions, time, certainty, and emphasis? Report an unresolved meaning question instead of accepting a fluent guess. For a language review, identify the passage, consequence, and justified repair.
