# Purpose, Audience & Context

Use to choose purpose, audience, context, medium, scope, and depth. Reuse established decisions; no questionnaire or separate brief is required.

## Define purpose and speaker

Identify what readers should understand, decide, do, or experience. "Migration" is a topic; deciding whether to migrate and performing a migration require different content.

Distinguish the reader's need from the publisher's objective. A launch announcement can promote a product while giving readers enough information to assess its relevance and limits. Resolve important conflicts without silently dropping a requirement or a fact readers need for their decision.

Choose the primary outcome when goals compete, retaining secondary goals required by the brief. Reflection, fiction, and other experiential work may intentionally delay a conclusion or preserve ambiguity. A private journal need not acquire an external audience or call to action.

Establish who the text represents when it affects meaning: an individual, team, organization, narrator, or another requested role. A personal suggestion and an official instruction carry different commitments.

## Assess reader knowledge

Identify the primary reader and any secondary readers whose needs change the content. Consider topic expertise, project familiarity, language proficiency, practical experience, questions, objections, and reasons for reading. Use evidence relevant to this piece.

Do not substitute demographic labels or job titles for knowledge of readers. An experienced engineer can be new to this project; a specialist reading in another language may need full technical depth with clearer language.

Compare required knowledge with what readers can reasonably know. Explain missing terms, relationships, or reasoning; add examples when useful. Keep familiar precise terms and omit background that does not help the task.

## Ask, research, or proceed

Use the brief, existing material, correspondence, reader questions, support feedback, observations, or relevant research when available. Inspect inputs likely to change the writing decision. Do not claim reader research that did not occur.

Distinguish supplied or observed facts, interpretations, and unknowns. A brief defines the assignment, not every reader's needs. Usage data alone cannot show understanding; experts may not represent newcomers. Imagined personas and model-generated reactions are hypotheses.

- Ask when plausible interpretations change the audience, claim, required action, commitment, or essential coverage and context provides no defensible default.
- Proceed when a reasonable default supports useful work. Disclose an assumption when it materially affects the result.
- Leave an affected claim or section provisional when a missing fact determines its scope or consequences; complete independent parts.
- Investigate when the uncertainty warrants research within the authorized task. A short edit does not require an audience interview or new research project.

For a requested draft or reusable template, use descriptive placeholders for required missing values when useful, for example [release date]. Distinguish them from literal text. For finished copy, resolve required gaps or identify what remains incomplete; do not present placeholders as verified facts.

## Match genre, medium, and context

Consider urgency, available attention, earlier communication, point of use, access needs, device, language, and whether the content may be forwarded or read independently.

Distinguish the type of writing from its delivery medium. An email can contain an announcement, apology, or decision memo; the channel alone does not determine formality, length, or structure. Use project examples and the requested outcome to interpret expectations.

Respond to known circumstances without assuming readers' emotions. A failed transaction needs an explanation and next step, but does not reveal how every reader feels. Use [Voice & Tone](voice-and-tone.md) when this affects expression.

Respect the requested medium. When it is open, choose for the actual use: immediate exchange, persistent lookup, close reading, learning, or another purpose. Digital content can need depth. Propose a consequential format change instead of silently replacing the deliverable.

## Choose depth and scope

Decide the coverage, explanation, practical detail, and decision support the outcome requires:

| Need | Include |
| --- | --- |
| Understand a mechanism | Necessary concepts, relationships, and an example when it explains the mechanism |
| Carry out a task | Prerequisites, actions, important conditions, expected results, and relevant recovery |
| Make a decision | Comparable options, evidence, consequences, limitations, and trade-offs |
| Find a fact | A direct answer with the qualifications needed to interpret it |
| Experience a narrative or reflection | Detail, pacing, and uncertainty appropriate to the intended effect |

Add explanations where knowledge is missing; remove help that gets in the way. Experts may need unfamiliar constraints explained. Newcomers may need one specific answer rather than a field introduction.

State exclusions only when readers could reasonably expect the omitted material. For mixed audiences, prioritize the intended reader and keep essential information available to other legitimate recipients. Summaries, optional detail, links, or separate treatments can address different needs; do not produce multiple versions automatically.

Distinguish learning a skill from applying existing knowledge. A tutorial and how-to guide can serve different needs without representing beginner and advanced versions of the same text. Do not force documentation categories onto letters, essays, or fiction.

If a length limit cannot accommodate a decisive condition or explanation, identify the conflict rather than omit it silently.

## Check audience fit

Check decisions actually made: does the piece serve the requested outcome, assume appropriate knowledge, and remain understandable at its point of use? Could a forwarded message or section entry lose necessary context?

When relevant reader feedback is available, check whether readers can explain the intended meaning, make the intended judgment, or complete the task. Use suitable criteria for experiential work. A readability score, imagined persona, or favorable model review does not prove reader understanding.

For an audience-fit review, explain what does not fit, why it matters, the evidence or assumption behind the finding, and what remains unknown.
