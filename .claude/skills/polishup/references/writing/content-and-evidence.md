# Content & Evidence

Use to develop content, assess reasoning, evaluate sources, check important claims, combine findings, or preserve source meaning. Reuse the purpose and scope. Fiction, a summary limited to supplied sources, and a researched recommendation need different checks.

## Develop useful content

Identify the question, problem, observation, or experience being addressed. Develop it by explaining how something works, what differs, how options compare, what follows, or why it matters.

If a passage is generic, identify the missing information: actors, conditions, explanation of an outcome, a relevant example, or why a distinction matters. Develop that information instead of adding adjectives or restating the conclusion. An example explains a possibility; it does not establish its frequency.

Use supplied material to develop ideas. If it cannot answer an important question, narrow the answer, identify the gap, or seek information when warranted. Do not invent findings, customer experiences, or technical detail to imply depth or originality. A practical reply can simply answer correctly; reflective work need not contain an academic thesis.

## Connect claims to support

For an important claim, identify the evidence, how it supports the conclusion, and any required assumptions. Distinguish observations, interpretations, recommendations, personal judgments, and hypothetical examples. Label them only when their status is unclear.

Check common inference errors:

- Association or a before/after change alone does not establish causation.
- One example does not establish a general rule.
- Evidence that an option works does not establish that it is best.
- A recommendation also depends on goals, costs, values, and alternatives.

Consider a plausible competing explanation or objection when it could change the conclusion. Compare fairly; do not create a weak opponent or give unsupported positions equal weight. If a strong objection remains, narrow the claim or state the unresolved choice. Correct the reasoning rather than only softening the wording. A formal argument template is optional.

## Evaluate sources

Prioritize verification by consequence, uncertainty, changeability, and importance to the answer. Check consequential claims before polishing peripheral wording. A wording-only edit does not automatically require a research project.

Match evidence to the question. Original records establish what was stated or specified; a sound synthesis may answer a broad research question better than one isolated study. A vendor can document its product without independently establishing superiority. Reputation, peer review, recency, and citation count are signals, not proof of claim support.

For an unfamiliar source, check who produced it, their relevant knowledge, methods, incentives, and independent accounts. Trace important claims to the evidence. Three articles repeating one press release are not independent sources. Check dates, versions, corrections, populations, and scope; publication, event, and data-collection dates can differ.

Read the relevant material. Snippets and generated summaries provide leads but can omit qualifications. Restrict claims to accessible evidence; disclose abstract-only or excerpt-only access when it affects the conclusion. Without retrieval, use supplied material and identify verification gaps. Never invent a source or claim to have opened it.

Stop searching when the scoped claims have adequate support and more research is unlikely to change the answer. When support remains missing, qualify, omit, narrow, or leave the affected part provisional. Ask for missing information when it determines the deliverable.

## Synthesize information

Organize around the question, comparing agreement, disagreement, definitions, populations, periods, outcomes, and methods. Explain what follows from those relationships rather than listing successive summaries.

When comparison needs it, record important claims and supporting passages in existing notes or tools. No dedicated ledger is required. Distinguish source statements from your interpretation.

Investigate apparent contradictions. Different measurements or conditions may explain them. If evidence remains incompatible, state the disagreement and its consequence. Do not average incomparable findings, count copied sources as corroboration, or suppress counterevidence.

## Check facts and numbers

Verify consequential names, dates, quotations, attributed statements, and factual details against relevant records. Keep claims attached to the applicable population, place, product version, and period.

For numerical claims, check unit, denominator, baseline, timeframe, sample, comparison, and evidence quality. Recalculate derived values with suitable tools when useful. Distinguish:

- Counts from rates.
- Relative percentage change from percentage-point change.
- Observations from predictions.
- Statistical uncertainty from practical importance.

A decline from 20% to 15% is 5 percentage points, or a 25% relative decline if the measurements are comparable. It does not alone establish a causal effect or a company-wide result.

Use only justified precision. Even a correct number can mislead without its baseline, denominator, scope, or limitations.

## Explain uncertainty

Specify what is uncertain: measurement, limited coverage, disputed interpretation, prediction, or incomplete verification. Explain the implication for the conclusion. Replace repeated vague hedges with an available range, condition, date, or attribution; do not invent probabilities or confidence scores.

State supported facts directly and retain qualifications that limit them. Failure to retrieve evidence is not proof that a claim is false or that evidence does not exist. Uncertainty belongs with the claim it limits, not only in a distant disclaimer.

## Attribute sources and quotations

Use links, named sources, notes, or formal citations as the genre requires. Consequential or disputed factual claims need traceability; common knowledge, preferences, and clearly fictional events do not all need academic references.

Verify both the source identity and its support for the attached claim. Never invent authors, dates, titles, URLs, quotations, or page numbers. If verification is incomplete, use verified support, narrow the claim, or state the gap.

Preserve direct quotations and distinguish omissions, alterations, and translations when relevant. Do not convert an illustrative example into a reported event or invent testimony or lived experience for factual writing. Hypothetical or fictional material is appropriate when requested and its status is clear.

## Preserve meaning when summarizing or revising

Check source fidelity, whether the text preserves the source's meaning, separately from factual accuracy. A faithful summary can repeat an error; a plausible addition can misrepresent the source. Stay within permitted sources. Flag errors or clearly separate authorized corrections instead of silently adding outside information.

Compare important statements and their relationships: actor, action, recipient, condition, time, certainty, negation, attribution, comparison direction, and restrictions. Preserve these relationships even when sentence structure changes. Do not turn a possibility into a guarantee, a proposal into a commitment, or an attributed claim into an established fact.

Check omissions as well as sentences. Dropping an exception, counterfinding, or condition can make an otherwise supported summary misleading. If the limit cannot preserve the essential meaning, identify the trade-off. An appealing rewrite or fluent translation does not establish fidelity.

For creation, resolve consequential gaps before asserting conclusions. For refinement, repair the unsupported claim, inference, or meaning change and retain sound content. For review, distinguish unsupported, contradicted, and not yet verified; identify the affected claim and what would resolve it. Confident recall, repeated model agreement, and a genuine citation are not independent verification.
